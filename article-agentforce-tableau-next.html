Your AI is bad at math. The Tableau Next analytics agent isn't.

Your agent answers a lot of different questions. Some are about a record or a document. Some are math across thousands of rows. Those are two different jobs, and getting an answer you can trust means each one goes to the right engine. Here's how that works, and how to check it yourself.

The short version

Agentforce finds records, takes actions, and reads documents. Tableau Next computes answers over your governed data model. When a question needs math across a set of rows (count, sum, trend, compare, rank), it goes to the analytics engine so the number is calculated, not estimated. One conversation, the right engine behind each answer.

01

Why the routing matters: the two engines answer different question shapes. Lookups and actions are a reasoning job. Counts, sums, and trends are a calculation job. Sending each question to the engine built for it is what makes the analytical answers exact and repeatable instead of approximate.

How Agentforce handles a question

Built for finding things, doing things, and summarizing things. The work happens inside the language model, reasoning over what it retrieved.

1
Reads the request and grounds on your data.For records it queries your CRM objects directly. For documents and knowledge it retrieves the relevant passages for the model to read.
2
Hands the retrieved material to the model.The records and passages become the context the model reads and reasons over.
3
Reasons and writes a clear answer.Ideal for "find this account," "draft this email," "summarize this case." It's reasoning over what it was handed.
4
For questions that need calculation, it routes.When the question is "count," "sum," or "trend" across many rows, that's a calculation job, and it goes to the analytics engine rather than being approximated.
The right fit for: finding a record, acting on a record (create, update), and summarizing a bounded set of records or documents. If the answer lives in a specific record or a readable passage, this is the engine.

How Tableau Next handles the same question

Built for the math. The model decides what to ask for. Software does the calculation.

1
Maps the question to your governed model.Metrics and dimensions are defined once by your team ("revenue," "active client," "relationship type"). If a definition is missing, it asks rather than guessing.
2
Describes the intent as a structured request.Dimensions, measures, filters, against the model. The model decides what to ask for, not how to compute it.
3
The engine calculates the answer.It runs the grouping, the sums, the joins, in software, across the full data set, not a sample.
4
Returns an exact answer and a chart.Same question, same governed answer, every time, traced back to a definition your team owns.
The right fit for: aggregation, trends, comparisons, ranking, anything that's "do the math across many records and show me." The answer is calculated, consistent, and traceable to a definition.
Neither engine is "better." They're built for different question shapes, and they're strongest when each one only does the job it's good at. The next section is why that division is what makes the analytical answers hold up.
04

Why the analytical answers hold up

This is the part worth understanding, because it's the reason the numbers are trustworthy and not just confident. The short answer: the agent stopped asking the AI to do the part AI is bad at. Don't take our word for it. Ask the model directly.

ai-agent · chat
>Are you good at math?
AI agent

Honestly? Not the way you'd want for anything that has to be exact. I'm good at understanding what you're asking and setting up how to solve it. I'm not reliable at actually doing the arithmetic, especially over a lot of data.

Hand me a few numbers and I can usually add them. But ask me to total something across thousands of rows and I'm working from what fits in front of me and estimating the rest. It'll read as exact. It might not be. And you wouldn't be able to tell.

So for anything that counts, the right move isn't to trust my mental math. It's to let me figure out precisely what you're asking, then have that computed for real, the same way every time. I'm better as the part that understands the question than the part that crunches it.

Same question, same chat window. The difference is where the math happens: inside the model on the left, in a calculation engine on the right. That's why one answer wavers and the other holds.
That difference comes down to one design choice: neither part is asked to do the other's job.
Understanding the question goes to the AI, which is what it's good at. Computing the answer goes to a calculation engine running against a semantic model your team defines. The AI never does the arithmetic, so the answer can't drift.

This is the work, and it's also where the payoff comes from. The answers are only as good as the model behind them. Build that model well once, and every analytical answer your team asks for is grounded in it. The trustworthiness comes from the modeling, not from a cleverer prompt.
▸Go one level deeperfor the technically curious

There's a reason it maps your question to the right numbers so reliably, and it happens before anyone asks anything. The engine studies your semantic model first. When your team publishes the model, the engine reads its structure: the tables, how they relate, and the metrics your team has defined. It builds an understanding of your business concepts up front.

So when someone later asks for "commercial pipeline," the engine isn't guessing what that phrase points to in the moment. It already knows which defined metric that maps to, because it studied the model ahead of time. That up-front grounding is what lets a vague, human phrase land on the exact governed number every time.

This is also why curating the semantic model is worth doing carefully. The richer and clearer your definitions, the more natural language the agent can map without asking. The modeling work compounds: it's a layer you build once and every future answer draws on.
